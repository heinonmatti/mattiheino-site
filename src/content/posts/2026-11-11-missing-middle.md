## The workshop

One thread ran through the whole workshop: whom people lean on when things go wrong. The new survey scale from the Dutch public health institute RIVM measures feeling prepared on seven dimensions, one of them social – for instance, whether you could easily get practical help from your neighbours. Portugal reached people in the pandemic through those they already trusted, training thousands of firefighters, police officers and pharmacists as "micro-influencers" and even asking them to record the doubts they cleared up in passing at work. In Valencia, more than half of those affected by the 2024 floods got no alert, and people trusted family, friends and volunteers more than the institutions. For women who gave birth in Lebanon during or after the 2024 war, the speaker called for psychosocial support and for midwives and nurses trained to act when things go wrong in a crisis.

This is the middle I talked about: the networks between households and institutions, which filter official guidance and hold each other up when an emergency hits. In the discussion, the Valencia speaker said the authorities must involve communities in designing and carrying out their protocols. I added a point from [an interview study](https://web.archive.org/web/20250418080028/https://api.hankeikkuna.fi/asiakirjat/2a086d9c-2eef-454c-b430-1a7cc5aff6d9/f9f5cada-4927-4a06-b287-57ff9c5b4747/RAPORTTI_20230403105040.pdf) we did at the Finnish Prime Minister's Office, which recommended making clear who does what: many people expect someone to bring them water and food, and when no one comes, the broken trust is the wet dream of any hybrid influencer – "I told you, you can't trust them!"

In Finland, [the new official community guidance](https://kylavara.fi/2026/10/07/kylavara-local-community-preparedness/) offers a scaffold to building the middle: e.g. a housing association member can use it to start a conversation about preparedness without anyone being taken for a tinfoil hat wearer. In Finnish, we have a proverb "The opportunity makes a thief". This also works the positive way: Making preparedness and other community activities easy, can reel people in when they'd otherwise lack the motivation.

## Footnote: the Commission's preparedness guidelines of 30 October 2026

Two weeks before the talk, the European Commission published its [Guidelines on Population Preparedness under the EU Preparedness Union Strategy](https://ec.europa.eu/echo/files/policies/preparedness/guidelines_population_preparedness_en.pdf). On slide 12, which sets the Strategy's key actions against the scales of society, the middle – neighbourhood, town and region – holds a single action: the EU Preparedness Day. The guidelines name the middle more often. The advice on 72-hour self-sufficiency calls for "encouraging cooperation and mutual support among households and communities", and preparedness education is to go into existing curricula "while enabling schools to act as community resilience hubs". The guidelines are also non-binding by their own account, meant "to encourage" member states to act "according to their own national organisation". They set no EU target and commit no EU money; the "adequate financing" they mention is for national and local governments to provide.
